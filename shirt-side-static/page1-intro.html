A tutorial, not a guess

Print a shirt that survives the wash.

Silkscreen is one stencil, one ink color, and a squeegee. The part that ruins shirts is quieter: emulsion exposed for someone else’s lamp, and ink that looked dry and was not cured.

One screen, one color

A second ink is a second film and a second screen, lined up with registration marks.

Do not copy a timer

Exposure minutes from a video are that person’s lamp. Run a step test on yours.

Dry is not cured

Plastisol fuses with heat through the whole film. Water-based shop ink has to dry before it can cure.

What the videos leave out

The tutorial follows shop practice from ScreenPrinting.com, Practical Textile, and Catspit Productions, then checks it against the YouTube lessons people actually watch.

Exposure time

ShopsScreenPrinting.com and Practical Textile say the time belongs to your lamp, emulsion, and mesh. Find it with a step test.

On YouTubeI Like To Make Stuff uses a bright lamp for roughly 30–45 minutes. A home kit elsewhere quotes three minutes with a halogen lamp and a minute of daylight.

Cure

ShopsPlastisol has to reach fusion temperature through the whole film, often 320°F, measured with a probe. An iron is not that tool.

On YouTubeThe same home video heat-sets shirts in a clothes dryer. Speedball Flex, a different ink, does publish an iron method at 300°F.

Stencil

ShopsPhoto emulsion is the stencil you can print, clean, and print again.

On YouTubeCricut and JenniferMaker teach a vinyl stencil. It is a real way to learn the stroke, and it is a short run.
